# Cover Letter and CV Format: What I Recommend and Why

# Why the Combined Single-Document Format

The format choice is small but consequential. Most readers default to either two separate files (cover letter `.docx` plus resume `.docx`, sometimes both attached to the same email) or a one-page resume with no cover letter. Neither serves you well in a brutal market.

**Two separate files** asks the recruiter to open two attachments. In practice, half of recruiters skim the resume and never read the cover letter. The cover letter you spent forty minutes tailoring loses its job. The signal is weak because the channel is weak.

**Resume only** removes the cover letter entirely, which sounds efficient until you remember that the cover letter is the part of the application where you get to control the framing. Without it, the recruiter is left to interpret your resume cold. For senior roles where the framing matters more than the line items, this is a missed opportunity every single time.

**The combined single document** puts the cover letter on page 1 and the resume on page 2 (and occasionally page 3). One attachment. One file the recruiter opens and reads in sequence. The cover letter sets the framing in the first ten seconds. The resume confirms it in the next thirty.

There is a second, less obvious benefit. When you combine the documents, you can use the same header table across both pages: name, headline, contact links. The recruiter sees your name and headline four times in a single open (top of page 1, top of page 2, optionally top of page 3, and the closing signature on page 1). That repetition matters because the recruiter is screening dozens of applications in a single sitting. Anything that helps them remember which person is which is anchoring you in their working memory.

The combined format also forces an editorial discipline that helps the application overall. Cover letter and resume have to feel like the same person wrote them, in the same voice, in the same week. Putting them in one document makes voice drift impossible to ignore.

# Recruiter Time Math and the Two-Page Target

The time math is the underrated case for the combined format.

Recruiter screening data has been studied for over a decade, and the consensus number across multiple eye-tracking studies is around seven to ten seconds for the initial scan. That number is for resumes alone, without cover letters. The recruiter is looking at the top third of page 1, scanning for name, headline, current role, current company, and one or two keywords. If you make it past the initial scan, they spend another twenty to forty seconds before deciding to advance.

What this means for the combined format: page 1 (your cover letter) gets the initial ten-second scan. The recruiter reads the opener, the bolded reasons, and the closing signature. If the opener lands and the reasons match the role, they flip to page 2 (your resume) for the confirmation pass. The resume's job at that point is to support the cover letter's claims with specific bullets and metrics.

Two pages is the target because it matches the time recruiters actually spend. Three pages is acceptable when the candidate's experience genuinely needs the room (senior plus roles with multi-decade tenure, or roles where the third page is filled with shipped work, publications, or patents that strengthen the application). One page is fine for under five years of experience.

Four pages is too many. Five is far too many. Recruiters do not read past page three for an initial screen, ever. If your application is sprawling, the back pages are decoration; cut them.

A practical heuristic: write the application as if the reader will stop at the end of page 2. Page 3 is a bonus. Anything important must land on page 1 or page 2.

# The Template Structure

The template ships as an `.docx` file with the structure already in place. The skill drops your tailored content into the template; you do not have to assemble the layout yourself. Here is what is in the file.

**Header table (top of every page).** A two-column table with your name on the left (large, bold), and headline on the right (medium, bold). Below the headline, three or four contact links (small): email, LinkedIn URL, GitHub or portfolio URL, optionally a city. The table has no visible borders. It looks like a header, not a table.

**Page 1: Cover letter.**

- Date and addressee (the company name and role title, sometimes a hiring manager name if you have one).
- Opener: one sharp sentence. Signals the match, the seniority, the specific angle you bring. This sentence does real work.
- Three or four bolded reasons. Each reason is a short bold one-liner followed by a one- or two-sentence justification grounded in a specific past role, metric, or shipped product. The reasons are sequenced from strongest match to second-strongest match.
- Closing paragraph. Two or three sentences. Direct. No throat-clearing.
- Signature: your name, role title, contact links.

**Page 2 and beyond: Resume.**

- Header table repeats (same as page 1).
- Core Competencies: one wrapped paragraph block, capped at three lines. Five competencies, all defensible, all matched to the Job Description (JD).
- Experience section: roles in reverse chronological order. Each role has a one-line header (company, role title, dates, location), followed by three to five bullets. The most recent role gets the most bullets. Older roles get fewer. Bullets are sentence-case, end with periods, and lead with the verb. Each bullet has either a specific product, a specific metric, or a specific decision; never all three at once.
- Education and Certifications: terminal degrees first, then certifications. Lapsed certifications are past-tense.
- Optional: Patents, Publications, Talks, Open Source. Only include if relevant to the role.
- Optional: Languages. Only include if relevant or if the role specifies a language requirement.

**Footer (every page).** Page number on the right. Nothing else. No company name. No date. No copyright.

**Margins and typography.** Margins are 0.6 to 0.8 inches all around. Tighter than the Word default of 1 inch. The savings buy you about ten percent more content per page without looking cramped. Font is a neutral sans-serif at 10.5 or 11 point for body text, 13 to 16 point for the name in the header, 10 point bold for section headers. Line spacing is 1.15 for body text. Avoid font weight extremes (no extra-bold, no light); they fail to render consistently when the recipient opens the document in their reader of choice.

# Google Docs Polish Checklist

The Google Docs polish step catches what Claude misses. It is the last ten percent. Skip it and you ship applications with avoidable mechanical issues. Here is the checklist I run on every document, in order.

**1. Open the `.docx` in Google Docs (or Microsoft Word desktop).** Not in the Markdown preview. Not in the Claude chat. In a real document editor where the rendering matches what the recruiter will see.

**2. Check the page count.** Target two pages. Three is acceptable if needed. If it is four or more, cut content.

**3. Check the page break between cover letter and resume.** The cover letter should end cleanly at the bottom of page 1. If the cover letter spills onto page 2 by a single line, tighten the cover letter (a sentence, a comma, a word). If the cover letter ends with too much white space at the bottom of page 1, lengthen one bolded reason or add a fourth reason.

**4. Read the opener of the cover letter three times.** The opener is the highest-leverage sentence in the document. Read it as if you were the recruiter. Does it land? Does it answer "why should I keep reading?" If not, ask Claude for three alternatives and pick the best, or write a new opener yourself.

**5. Check every bolded reason.** Is each reason grounded in a specific role, metric, or product? Are the reasons sequenced strongest first? Are the justifications one or two sentences, not three or four?

**6. Check the resume Core Competencies.** Is it one wrapped block, capped at three lines? Are all five competencies defensible in an interview? Are they matched to the Job Description?

**7. Check every bullet.** Read each bullet aloud (in your head is fine). Does each one have a specific product, metric, or decision? Are there generic bullets ("collaborated cross-functionally") that could be cut? Is the recency curve right (more bullets on the most recent role, fewer on older roles)?

**8. Check the headline in the header table.** The headline should match the role you are applying for. If the Job Description says Director of Product Payments and your headline says Lead Product Manager, broaden the headline temporarily or add a sub-line. The header is anchoring the recruiter; do not waste it.

**9. Check the dates.** Reverse chronological order. Months and years for all roles. "Present" for the current role.

**10. Spot-check every metric.** Every number on the resume must be defensible in an interview. Cross-reference with your `6 Achievements.md` file. If a number was introduced during the draft conversation and is not yet in the Achievements file, that is a flag: either you have a new metric to add to the Brain Trust, or the number is wrong.

**11. Read everything out loud once.** Stilted sentences, awkward phrases, words you would never use in conversation: these are the things that survive an AI draft and only get caught by reading aloud. Rewrite anything that does not sound like you.

**12. Check the footer.** Page number on every page. Nothing else.

**13. Spell check and grammar check.** Run the document editor's checker. Accept fixes that are real. Reject fixes that change voice (the grammar checker does not understand your voice).

**14. Check the file name.** The file should be named `[Your Name] - [Role Title].pdf` unless the company specifies otherwise in the application form.

The checklist takes about eight to ten minutes if everything is in good shape, longer if rewrites are needed. Do not skip steps. Each one catches a specific failure mode that does happen.

# PDF Export Settings

The PDF is what the recruiter sees. The `.docx` is your working file. The PDF must look identical to what you see in the editor.

**File menu, Export, PDF Document.** Standard.

**Embed all fonts.** Google Docs and Microsoft Word both do this by default in modern versions. If you are using an older version of Word, double-check the embed-fonts setting in the export dialog.

**Use the highest-quality setting.** Smaller PDF file size is not worth losing crispness on the headline or the bullet markers. A two-page PDF will be well under 500 KB even at maximum quality.

**Do not flatten or password-protect.** Applicant Tracking Systems sometimes fail on flattened or protected PDFs. Send a plain PDF.

**Open the exported PDF and look at it.** Every page. Every line. Look for: shifted text, missing characters, wrong fonts, header table misalignment. If anything looks different from the `.docx`, the export failed and you need to re-export.

**Open the PDF on a different device if you can.** A PDF that looks fine on your laptop sometimes renders differently on a mobile device or a different operating system. If the recruiter opens your PDF on their phone and the header table is broken, you lose. A quick check on your phone or a colleague's machine catches this.

# Archetype Variants

The format stays the same across archetypes. The voice changes. Tell the skill the archetype explicitly in the clarifying-question step and the draft will match.

**Banking and regulated industries (formal).** Longer cover letter (closer to one full page). Credentialed opener that signals fit with the regulatory environment. Specific named compliance frameworks if you have shipped against them. Past employers named with full formal names, not abbreviations. Sentences are slightly longer, slightly more measured. Bullets reference frameworks, programs, governance bodies. No bullet lists in the cover letter; prose with bolded reasons.

**Startups and growth companies (punchy).** Shorter cover letter (closer to half a page). Direct opener that signals "I ship." Concrete shipped products, not "I led" or "I owned." Sentences are shorter. Verbs do more work. Bullets reference specific products by name and specific outcomes by number. The voice is closer to a builder telling you what they built.

**Big tech (crisp, metrics-heavy).** Medium-length cover letter. Opener references scale or the specific technical problem space. Bullets in both the cover letter justifications and the resume lean heavily on metrics: numbers of users, revenue impact, latency improvements, accuracy gains. The voice is precise and measured; over-the-top language is rejected. Three or four metrics on the most recent role, two or three on older roles.

**Other archetypes you may build over time.** Mission-driven nonprofit or impact. Government or regulatory. Health-tech. Education. Climate. Developer-facing platforms. Each one is a variant of the same format with a different voice register, anchored in your Cover Letters archetype files.

# ATS Considerations and What Breaks Them

Applicant Tracking Systems (ATS) are the software layer that sits between the recruiter and your application. The recruiter reads what the ATS parsed. If the ATS misparsed your document, the recruiter is reading a degraded version of your work.

Modern ATS handle `.docx` and `.pdf` well in most cases. The failures that still happen are predictable and avoidable.

**Tables in the body of the resume break ATS parsing.** A two-column resume layout, common in modern Word templates, often parses as a single long string where the left-column content is interleaved with the right-column content. Avoid two-column layouts. The header table in our template is fine because it is at the top and only has two cells; the ATS treats it as a label-value pair.

**Headers and footers in the document chrome (the area outside the body) can be ignored by ATS.** If you put your contact information in the document header or footer using Word's "Insert Header" feature, some ATS will miss it entirely. Keep contact links in the body of the document, inside the header table we use, not in the document chrome.

**Text boxes and floating shapes break ATS parsing.** If your cover letter has a decorative element (a colored sidebar, a callout box, a curved line), it is likely a floating shape. Most ATS skip floating shapes. Anything in a floating shape is invisible. Use only inline elements.

**Non-standard fonts can fail.** Stick to common sans-serif and serif fonts. Arial, Calibri, Helvetica, Inter, Times New Roman, Garamond. Avoid custom or paid fonts unless you have tested them with an ATS.

**Images of text are completely invisible to ATS.** If your name is rendered as an image (some designer-built templates do this), the ATS sees nothing. Use real text.

**Filename does not matter for ATS parsing.** But it matters for the recruiter who downloads your file. Use `[Your Name] - [Role Title].pdf`.

**Smart quotes and special characters are mostly fine in modern ATS.** The exception is anything outside basic Latin range. Em dashes, en dashes, dashes inside compound words, fancy bullets, and decorative dingbats can occasionally parse oddly. Stick to standard punctuation and bullets.

The general principle: simpler documents parse better. The template you are using is intentionally plain because plain is what survives the ATS layer. Keep it that way.
