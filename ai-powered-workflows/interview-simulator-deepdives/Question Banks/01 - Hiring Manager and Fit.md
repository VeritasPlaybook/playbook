# Hiring Manager and Fit: Question Bank

# What this round is really testing

This round makes one decision: would this person, at this level, on my team, make my next two quarters easier or harder. Everything else is downstream. The hiring manager is not sampling your career. They are testing whether the shape of your last job matches the open job, and whether you will need managing or do the managing.

It is usually run by the person you would report to, sometimes by that person's manager when the role backfills someone senior, or by a functional lead when the team is new. The distinction matters: a direct manager tests execution and working style, a skip level tests judgment and scope. If your intake email did not say which you are getting, assume direct manager and prepare one level up.

The failure modes are specific. First, the career montage: asked why this role, you answer with a chronological tour from your first job. Second, level mismatch, where you describe work a rung below the posting and the manager quietly reclassifies you. Third, the moving-away answer, where every reason for wanting the job is a complaint about the current one.

The fourth is the one nobody sees coming. A hiring manager is imagining the weekly one to one with you. If your answers suggest that meeting will be spent unblocking you, explaining context, or refereeing your conflicts, you lose the round on likability grounds nobody will ever write down.

Your questions count for real here. The hiring manager is the only interviewer who knows the honest state of the team and has an incentive to tell you, because they do not want you to quit in month four. Weak questions read as low interest.

# How to use this bank

Copy the probes into the question bank section of your simulator prompt, identifiers exactly as written. They keep your run log readable across sessions: a debrief that says "weak on HM-14 and HM-22" is actionable, one that says "weak on motivation" is not.

Run each session on the tag you have drilled least, not the one you enjoy. Start at difficulty tier one (T1), move to tier two (T2) once you can answer without a preamble, and reach for tier three (T3) probes only in the final two sessions before the round. Six to eight probes is a full session. Twenty is theatre.

Never paste the italic grading lines into the part of the prompt the simulator reads aloud. They are grader notes, and a simulator that recites them hands you the answer key.

# The probes

## Motivation

**HM-01 · motivation · T1**
"Why this role, and why now?"
*Strong answer contains:* a specific reason tied to this company's actual work, an honest account of what you are moving toward rather than away from, and a stop. No career montage.

**HM-02 · motivation · T1**
"Walk me through your background in about two minutes."
*Strong answer contains:* a through-line ending at this job, at most three roles named, one sentence per role saying what changed because you were there. Ends inside two minutes without being asked to stop.

**HM-03 · motivation · T2**
"What is it about our problem space specifically that interests you, as opposed to the ten other companies doing something similar?"
*Strong answer contains:* one concrete detail about the company's product, market, or constraint that could not be copy-pasted onto a competitor, plus a link to something the candidate has worked on.

**HM-04 · motivation · T2**
"You have been at your current employer for a while. What is unfinished there?"
*Strong answer contains:* an honest acknowledgement that something is unfinished, a reason the move still makes sense, and no disparagement of the current team.

**HM-05 · motivation · T2**
"If you got an offer from us and an offer from a larger, better known company at the same compensation, how would you decide?"
*Strong answer contains:* a decision criterion the candidate holds (scope, proximity to the problem, team, learning rate), applied out loud, rather than flattery.

**HM-06 · motivation · T3**
"What would have to be true about this job, twelve months in, for you to say it was the right move?"
*Strong answer contains:* two or three testable conditions, at least one about impact rather than personal growth, and a willingness to name what would make it the wrong move.

**HM-07 · motivation · T3**
"Be honest: is this a step up, a step sideways, or a step down for you?"
*Strong answer contains:* a direct answer, a defensible reason it is still the right move, and no relabelling of a sideways move as a promotion.

## Scope and level

**HM-08 · scope · T1**
"What was the scope of your last role: team size, budget, surface area, decision rights?"
*Strong answer contains:* concrete numbers or a concrete description of the surface owned, plus what the candidate decided alone versus recommended.

**HM-09 · scope · T2**
"Describe something you owned end to end. Where did your ownership start and where did it stop?"
*Strong answer contains:* an explicit boundary. The candidate names what was not theirs. Unbounded ownership claims read as inflation.

**HM-10 · scope · T2**
"This role is broader than what you describe doing today. What is your plan for the parts you have not done?"
*Strong answer contains:* an honest gap list, one analogous experience per gap, and a concrete ramp plan rather than a claim it will be fine.

**HM-11 · scope · T2**
"How much of what you described was you, and how much was the team?"
*Strong answer contains:* a clean split, credit given by name or role, and no retreat into "we" for everything. Over-claiming and total deflection both lose here.

**HM-12 · scope · T3**
"If I gave you this role plus one more area next year, which area would you want, and which would you refuse?"
*Strong answer contains:* an actual refusal. A candidate who wants everything is telling the manager they cannot prioritize.

**HM-13 · scope · T3**
"Who did you have to convince at the most senior level, and what happened in that room?"
*Strong answer contains:* a named seniority level, the specific objection raised, and what the candidate changed in response.

## Track record

**HM-14 · track-record · T1**
"Tell me about the work you are proudest of."
*Strong answer contains:* one project, the state before, the specific thing the candidate did, and the state after with a number or a verifiable outcome. Pride in a decision rather than a launch scores higher.

**HM-15 · track-record · T2**
"Tell me about a result you are proud of that took longer than you expected. What was the delay?"
*Strong answer contains:* a named cause of the delay that is not "stakeholders", what the candidate did once they saw it, and what they would build differently to catch it earlier.

**HM-16 · track-record · T2**
"What is a number from your work that you would defend under scrutiny, and how was it measured?"
*Strong answer contains:* the metric definition, the measurement method, the baseline, and the honest limits of the attribution.

**HM-17 · track-record · T2**
"What did you inherit that was broken, and what state was it in when you left it?"
*Strong answer contains:* a specific before and after, at least one thing that stayed broken, and why that one was correctly deprioritized.

**HM-18 · track-record · T3**
"Give me an example where your work did not move the number you were hired to move."
*Strong answer contains:* a real miss, the diagnosis, and what the candidate stopped doing as a result. Deflection to external causes without a self-directed lesson fails.

**HM-19 · track-record · T3**
"What in your resume would your last manager describe differently than you just did?"
*Strong answer contains:* a genuine difference in emphasis, stated without defensiveness, and a reason both accounts can be true.

## Working style

**HM-20 · working-style · T1**
"How do you like to be managed?"
*Strong answer contains:* a specific mechanism (cadence, what the candidate escalates, what they decide alone) rather than an adjective like "hands off". Names the failure mode they want their manager to catch.

**HM-21 · working-style · T1**
"What does your first ninety days look like here?"
*Strong answer contains:* a listening period with a defined end, one early deliverable that is useful even if the candidate's read is wrong, and a named ramp risk.

**HM-22 · working-style · T2**
"How do you decide what to escalate to me versus handle yourself?"
*Strong answer contains:* an explicit threshold (reversibility, blast radius, cost of delay) and one example either side of the line.

**HM-23 · working-style · T2**
"What kind of work do you avoid, and what happens to it when you avoid it?"
*Strong answer contains:* an honest aversion, the cost it has caused, and the mechanism the candidate uses to compensate.

**HM-24 · working-style · T2**
"Tell me about the working relationship in your career that was hardest to build. What made it hard?"
*Strong answer contains:* the candidate's own contribution to the difficulty, the concrete change they made, and where the relationship ended up.

**HM-25 · working-style · T3**
"My team is currently under-resourced and behind. If you joined tomorrow, what would you tell me to stop doing?"
*Strong answer contains:* a real recommendation with a stated basis, an acknowledgement of what the candidate does not yet know, and a cheap way to test the call before committing.

**HM-26 · working-style · T3**
"How would you handle it if I made a call you thought was wrong and did not change my mind?"
*Strong answer contains:* disagree and commit as behaviour rather than slogan: what the candidate would put in writing, what they would do publicly, and what trigger would make them raise it again.

## Judgment

**HM-27 · judgment · T2**
"Tell me about a decision you made with less information than you wanted."
*Strong answer contains:* what information was missing, the cheapest thing done to reduce uncertainty, the call, and how the candidate knew afterward whether it was right.

**HM-28 · judgment · T2**
"What is something you believe about your function that most people in it disagree with?"
*Strong answer contains:* an actual contested position, the evidence behind it, and the conditions under which the candidate would abandon it.

**HM-29 · judgment · T3**
"Say you inherit a roadmap you did not write and do not believe in. First month. What do you do?"
*Strong answer contains:* sequencing (learn before cutting), one thing kept deliberately to build credit, an explicit stakeholder path, and a stated cost of being wrong.

**HM-30 · judgment · T3**
"Give me an example where you were the only person who thought something was a problem. What happened?"
*Strong answer contains:* how the candidate tested whether they were wrong, how they raised it, and the outcome including the case where they were mistaken.

## Gaps and risk

**HM-31 · gaps · T2**
"Looking at this job description, where are you weakest?"
*Strong answer contains:* a real weakness relevant to the role, evidence the candidate has closed a comparable gap before, and no attempt to convert it into a strength.

**HM-32 · gaps · T3**
"What is the reason we might not hire you, in your own words?"
*Strong answer contains:* an accurate reading of the risk the panel is holding, delivered calmly, plus the mitigation. Naming a risk the panel had not considered and defusing it scores highest.

**HM-33 · gaps · T3**
"If you struggle in the first six months here, what will it have been?"
*Strong answer contains:* a specific, plausible failure mode tied to this environment, and the early signal the candidate would ask their manager to watch for.

## Closing and candidate questions

**HM-34 · closing · T1**
"What questions do you have for me?"
*Strong answer contains:* two or three questions that could only be asked of this manager (team's current constraint, how this role came to exist, what the last person in it found hard) and at least one that risks an uncomfortable answer.

**HM-35 · closing · T2**
"Is there anything we have not covered that you want me to know?"
*Strong answer contains:* one prepared item that fills a gap the round exposed, delivered in under a minute, not a summary of what was already said.

# The follow-ups they will actually use

A hiring manager rarely asks a second question from the list. They ask a second question about your answer. Load these escalations into your simulator so it does not let a first answer stand.

**"What was your part in that, specifically?"** Used whenever an answer arrives in the first person plural. It separates the candidate from the team.

**"Who disagreed with you, and what did they say?"** Used when a story has no friction. A decision nobody objected to was not a decision.

**"How did you know that was the right metric?"** Used when a number appears. Tests whether the candidate chose the measure or inherited it.

**"What would you do differently?"** Used after any success story. A candidate with nothing to change learned nothing.

**"What did that cost?"** Used after any win. Every real decision traded something away, and naming the trade is the level signal.

**"Say more about the part you skipped."** Used when a story jumps from problem to outcome. The skipped middle is usually where the work was.

**"Was that your call, or were you carrying someone else's call?"** Used to test decision rights against claimed scope.

**"How long did that take, start to finish?"** Used to size the work honestly. Impressive outcomes in implausible timeframes get audited.

**"And then what happened after you left?"** Used to test durability. Work that reverted the month the candidate left is a different result.

**"If I called your last manager, what would they say was hard about working with you?"** Used late, once rapport exists. It checks whether the candidate has an accurate self-model.

# Scoring dimensions for this round

Score each dimension one to five. Three is "acceptable at this level". Five is "I would open a headcount for this person". Two or below on two dimensions in the same run is a no-hire simulation, and the debrief should say so plainly.

**Level match.** Does the described scope, decision authority, and blast radius match the posted level, without inflation or underselling. This dimension most often decides the round and least often gets prepared for.

**Motivation specificity.** Is there a reason for this job at this company that would break if the company name were swapped. Generic enthusiasm scores two by definition.

**Evidence quality.** Are claims backed by before-and-after states, defensible numbers, and named constraints rather than adjectives. A candidate who can state how a metric was measured scores a full point above one who only quotes it.

**Self-awareness.** Can the candidate name a real weakness, a real miss, and their own contribution to a difficult relationship, without collapsing or converting it into a humblebrag.

**Manageability.** Would the weekly one to one be spent on the work or on the candidate. Signals: clear escalation thresholds, disagree and commit as behaviour, an honest ninety day plan.

**Communication and presence.** Answer length under control, a visible structure, an actual stop at the end, and steadiness under a follow-up that goes somewhere uncomfortable.

# Landmines

**The career montage.** Answering "why this role" with a chronological history. The manager stops listening around role two and starts drafting the rejection.

**Moving away, not moving toward.** Every stated reason is a complaint about the current job. Even accurate complaints read as a risk that they follow you here.

**Unbounded ownership claims.** Saying you owned something without naming where ownership stopped. Experienced managers read that as inflation and discount everything else.

**The "we" wall.** Refusing to separate your contribution from the team's after being asked twice. The first deflection is modesty and the second is a flag.

**Weakness theatre.** Offering "I care too much" or "I go too deep" as the honest gap. It costs a full point on self-awareness and invites a harder follow-up.

**Trashing the current employer.** Any specific criticism of a named former colleague. The manager is not evaluating your old team, they are watching how you will one day describe theirs.

**No stop.** Talking past the answer because the manager has not interrupted. Silence after a complete answer is normal interviewing behaviour, not a request for more.

**Overcorrecting to humility.** Undercutting your own work with "it was a small thing" or "anyone could have done it". Managers hiring at level need to see you hold a claim.

**No questions, or hygiene questions.** Asking only about process, tooling, or things the careers page answers. It reads as low interest at the moment interest is being measured.

**Pretending to know the company.** Repeating a marketing line back as insight. Managers hear their own slogan and mark the candidate as unprepared.
