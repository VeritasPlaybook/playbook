# Executive and Panel: Question Bank

# What this round is really testing

By the time you reach an executive or a panel, your ability to do the job is mostly settled. This room decides something narrower and harder to prepare for: whether they would put you in front of their own stakeholders, and whether hiring you creates risk they would have to manage personally.

Executives run this round on a different clock. They have limited context on you, limited time, and a strong preference for people who compress. A hiring manager will follow you through a five minute answer. An executive decides at ninety seconds whether the remaining three and a half minutes are worth their attention, and their body language tells you before their words do.

Panels change the physics further. Three people, three lenses, one hour, and a scorecard reconciled after you leave. Each panelist listens for their own dimension, and an answer that satisfies one can fail another. The commonest way to lose a panel is answering the technical panelist so thoroughly that the business panelist stops listening.

The first failure mode is the unabridged answer: correct content, three times too long. The second is the level slip, where a candidate interviewing for a leadership role answers with implementation detail because that is their most confident material. The third is treating the panel as an audience rather than three separate customers.

The fourth quietly ends most executive rounds. Asked what they would do about something the executive owns, the candidate produces a diplomatic non-answer. Executives are not testing tact, they assume you have it. They are testing whether you have a view and can hold it while being pushed.

# How to use this bank

Copy the probes into the question bank section of your simulator prompt, identifiers exactly as written. Give the simulator a hard length cap: it interrupts at ninety seconds on any answer and marks the interruption in the run log. The interruption count is the most useful number this bank produces.

Start at difficulty tier one (T1), move to tier two (T2) once you can answer without a preamble, and reach for tier three (T3) probes only in the final two sessions before the round.

Drill compression separately from content. Take an answer you already have and produce three versions: fifteen seconds, forty five seconds, two minutes. The fifteen second version is not a summary of the two minute version, it is the conclusion with one reason attached.

For a real panel, run at least two sessions where the simulator plays three interviewers with different lenses and switches between them without warning. Have it score each panelist separately, then reconcile, because that is what the room will do.

# The probes

## Vision and strategy

**EXEC-01 · vision · T1**
"What do you think we should be worried about in the next two years?"
*Strong answer contains:* one specific threat rather than a list, a reason it is underweighted rather than obvious, an acknowledgement of what the candidate cannot see from outside, and a stop.

**EXEC-02 · vision · T2**
"If you were running this function, what would you change first?"
*Strong answer contains:* a change with a stated basis, an explicit sequencing argument (why first), what the candidate would deliberately not touch in the first six months, and a way to test the call cheaply.

**EXEC-03 · vision · T2**
"Where does our advantage come from, and how durable is it?"
*Strong answer contains:* a mechanism rather than an attribute (something that compounds), an honest read on how easily it is copied, and a named condition that would erode it.

**EXEC-04 · vision · T3**
"Make the case that our current strategy is wrong."
*Strong answer contains:* a fair restatement of the strategy first, one structural objection rather than an execution complaint, the evidence that would settle it, and no hedging into flattery at the end.

**EXEC-05 · vision · T3**
"What is the thing everyone in your industry believes that you think is false?"
*Strong answer contains:* a genuinely contested position, the experience that produced it, the strongest counterargument stated fairly, and the conditions under which the candidate would abandon it.

## Business judgment

**EXEC-06 · business · T1**
"How does the work you do connect to how the company makes money?"
*Strong answer contains:* a chain from the candidate's work to a revenue or cost line with no missing links, and an honest statement of where attribution gets weak.

**EXEC-07 · business · T2**
"You have a budget increase of twenty percent or a headcount increase of twenty percent. Which do you take?"
*Strong answer contains:* a question about the constraint being solved, a choice, the reason flexibility or capacity matters more here, and what the candidate would give back if the increase were withdrawn next year.

**EXEC-08 · business · T2**
"What would you kill?"
*Strong answer contains:* a real candidate for termination, the criterion used, the people cost acknowledged, and the plan for the customers or teams still depending on it.

**EXEC-09 · business · T3**
"Give me the business case for the thing you most want to build, in the terms I care about."
*Strong answer contains:* the cost, the expected return with the assumption it hangs on, the time to signal, the downside if it fails, and a stated ask. Delivered in under two minutes without slides.

## Leadership

**EXEC-10 · leadership · T1**
"What kind of team do you build?"
*Strong answer contains:* a specific hiring bar and one tradeoff the candidate makes deliberately, an example of a hire that proved the model, and an example that did not.

**EXEC-11 · leadership · T2**
"Tell me about the hardest people decision you have made."
*Strong answer contains:* the decision owned in first person singular, the timeline including how long the candidate waited before acting, what they would do earlier next time, and no detail that identifies the person.

**EXEC-12 · leadership · T2**
"How do you handle it when your team is behind and the commitment cannot move?"
*Strong answer contains:* an early and specific renegotiation attempt, what got cut, how the team was protected from a repeat, and an honest account of what the crunch cost.

**EXEC-13 · leadership · T3**
"You disagree with a decision I have already made and announced. What do you do?"
*Strong answer contains:* the private raise before the public commit, the written record of the concern, full public support afterward, a named trigger for revisiting, and the line at which the candidate would escalate instead.

**EXEC-14 · leadership · T3**
"What is the standard you hold that people find difficult to work with?"
*Strong answer contains:* a real standard, a specific instance of friction it caused, what the candidate does to make it survivable for others, and no conversion of the answer into a strength.

## Risk

**EXEC-15 · risk · T2**
"What is the biggest risk in the plan you just described?"
*Strong answer contains:* the risk the candidate would most like not to mention, its early warning signal, the mitigation, and the residual risk that remains after mitigation.

**EXEC-16 · risk · T2**
"Tell me about a time you carried a risk that did not pay off."
*Strong answer contains:* the expected value reasoning at the time, who was informed, the loss, and the specific change to how the candidate now sizes and communicates risk.

**EXEC-17 · risk · T3**
"Something goes badly wrong in your area and it reaches the board. Walk me through your first day."
*Strong answer contains:* containment before explanation, a fact base with known unknowns marked, a single named owner, a communication cadence with a next update time, and the customer decision separated from the internal one.

**EXEC-18 · risk · T3**
"When have you told a senior leader something they did not want to hear?"
*Strong answer contains:* the setup, the exact framing used, the reaction, what changed, and evidence the relationship survived.

## Decision making

**EXEC-19 · decision · T2**
"Give me a decision you made that you would defend in front of this whole panel."
*Strong answer contains:* the options, the criterion, the call, the result, and the honest cost. Chosen for the panel's dimensions rather than the candidate's favourite story.

**EXEC-20 · decision · T2**
"How do you decide when to decide? What tells you it is time?"
*Strong answer contains:* an explicit rule based on the cost of delay versus the value of more information, a distinction between reversible and irreversible calls, and an example of each.

**EXEC-21 · decision · T3**
"Your data says one thing and three experienced people say the opposite. Choose."
*Strong answer contains:* an interrogation of both (what would make the data misleading, what would make the experience out of date), a choice, and a cheap test to settle it if one exists.

**EXEC-22 · decision · T3**
"What have you changed your mind about in the last year?"
*Strong answer contains:* something substantive rather than cosmetic, the specific evidence that moved the candidate, and what they now do differently.

## Compression

These train length control. Run them at the end of every session against answers you have already given at full length.

**EXEC-23 · compression · T1**
"Give me that in fifteen seconds."
*Strong answer contains:* the conclusion first, one reason, one number, and a stop. Not a faster version of the same narrative.

**EXEC-24 · compression · T1**
"In one sentence: why you?"
*Strong answer contains:* a claim that is specific to this role and would be false if said by most other candidates.

**EXEC-25 · compression · T2**
"Summarize your last three years for someone who has thirty seconds."
*Strong answer contains:* one through-line, one proof point, and the reason it points at this job. No chronology.

**EXEC-26 · compression · T2**
"If I only remember one thing from this conversation, what should it be?"
*Strong answer contains:* a single message the candidate has chosen in advance, phrased so someone else can repeat it accurately in a hiring debrief.

**EXEC-27 · compression · T2**
"Give me the recommendation without the reasoning. I will ask for the reasoning if I want it."
*Strong answer contains:* an actual recommendation delivered first, a pause, and no reflexive slide back into justification.

**EXEC-28 · compression · T3**
"I have five minutes, not thirty. What do we cover?"
*Strong answer contains:* the candidate taking control of the agenda, naming the two things worth the time, checking that those are the right two, and finishing early.

# Three interviewers, three lenses

A panel is not one interview with more people. It is three interviews running concurrently, each panelist scoring a different dimension while listening to the same answer. Build your simulator to model them separately.

**Identify the lens before you answer.** Most panels contain a version of these three: an executive or skip-level testing judgment and level, a functional peer testing whether your craft is real, and a partner from another function (engineering, design, data, finance, risk) testing whether you will be workable for them. The question tells you which. "How would you approach this" from a skip-level is a judgment question, and the same words from a peer are a craft question.

**Answer the asker first, then widen.** Give the person who asked the version they need, in about forty five seconds. Then add one sentence that lands for a second lens. That is what makes an answer survive the reconciliation meeting: two panelists can each write down something they valued.

**Do not let one lens eat the hour.** The failure pattern is a technical panelist finding a thread they enjoy and pulling it for twenty minutes while the other two disengage. It is your job, not theirs, to notice. To reclaim it: finish the thread, then say what the implication was for the business or the customer, which invites another lens back in.

**Address the quiet one deliberately.** In most panels one person says almost nothing. They are often the one writing the longest note. Make eye contact when the answer touches their area, and if there is time for your questions, direct one to them.

**Handle contradiction without picking a side.** Two panelists will occasionally disagree in front of you. Name it rather than pretending it did not happen, state which way you lean and on what basis, and acknowledge the condition under which the other view is right. Candidates who flatten the disagreement to keep the peace lose both scores.

**Repeat your message across lenses.** Panels reconcile by comparing notes. If all three heard a different version of who you are, you become hard to advocate for. Choose one claim before the round and make sure each panelist hears it in the vocabulary of their own lens.

# The follow-ups they will actually use

**"So what?"** The executive follow-up. Asked when an answer describes activity instead of consequence.

**"Give me the number."** Asked when a claim arrives without size. Not knowing precisely is fine, refusing to estimate is not.

**"What would you do?"** Asked after any analysis that stops short of a recommendation.

**"What is the risk in that?"** Asked right after a confident proposal, to see whether the candidate has priced the downside.

**"Who would be against this, and are they wrong?"** Tests whether the candidate has mapped the room they would be operating in.

**"Say that in fifteen seconds."** A compression test and a patience signal at once. Treat it as information.

**"What do you need from me?"** A real question about how the candidate would use an executive. A candidate who needs nothing will not escalate.

**"How would you know in ninety days whether this was working?"** Tests whether the candidate builds feedback into a plan or only builds the plan.

**"What is the second-order effect?"** Senior-level probe. Tests whether the candidate reasons past the immediate outcome.

**"Convince the person in this room who disagrees with you."** Live influence test, run in front of the other panelists.

# Scoring dimensions for this round

**Level and judgment.** Does the candidate operate at the altitude of the role, with reasoning that holds at that altitude rather than dropping into implementation detail for comfort.

**Compression.** Is the conclusion available in the first fifteen seconds, and does the answer end when it is finished. Track interruptions per session as the hard measure.

**Commercial reasoning.** Does the candidate connect their work to money, risk, or customer outcomes without being led there.

**Conviction under pressure.** Does the candidate hold a position when pushed, update when given new information, and distinguish between the two. Both immovability and instant collapse score low.

**Panel management.** Are all lenses served, is the quiet panelist reached, is a single consistent claim carried across the room.

**Communication and presence.** Steadiness, pace, comfort with silence, and the ability to be interrupted without losing the thread.

# Landmines

**The unabridged answer.** Correct content at three times the necessary length. The most reliable way to lose an executive round while saying nothing wrong.

**The diplomatic non-answer.** Refusing to take a position on something the executive owns. It reads as absence of a view, not as respect.

**Level slip.** Retreating into implementation detail because it is the most confident material available. It answers a question nobody asked and reclassifies the candidate downward.

**Playing to one panelist.** Building rapport with the friendliest person in the room and losing the other two. Reconciliation is where the decision is made.

**Flattery.** Praising the strategy, the product, or the panelist instead of engaging with the question. Executives hear it constantly and discount it instantly.

**Numbers with no size.** Talking about impact in adjectives. If the magnitude is not known, estimate out loud and label the estimate.

**Losing the thread after an interruption.** Being cut off mid-answer and either restarting or abandoning the point. Take the interruption, answer it, then return to the point in one sentence.

**Overreach.** Asserting things about the company's internal state that cannot be known from outside. One unsupportable claim in an executive room costs more than in any other round.

**Ignoring a stated constraint.** The executive says the budget is fixed and the answer requires more budget. It signals that direction is optional.

**No ask.** Finishing an executive conversation without a question that only an executive could answer. It is the last impression the room keeps.
