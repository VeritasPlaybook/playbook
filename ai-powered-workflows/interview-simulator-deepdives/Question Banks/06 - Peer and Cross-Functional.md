# Peer and Cross-Functional: Question Bank

# What this round is really testing

One question sits behind every probe in this bank and is almost never asked out loud: would I want this person in my week. The peer across from you is not deciding whether you are impressive. They are imagining a specific Tuesday six months from now when something is broken, the two of you disagree about why, and there is no manager in the room.

This round is run by someone at your level, usually from a partner function. A product manager is interviewed by an engineer or a designer, an engineer by a product manager or a data scientist, a marketer by a salesperson or an analyst. Their veto is real and often exercised quietly, through a scorecard comment rather than a stated objection.

Peers score differently from managers. A manager scans for capability and risk. A peer scans for friction: whether you show up with decisions already made, whether you hear a constraint the first time it is said, whether you take their work and present it as yours, and whether disagreeing with you will be expensive.

The first failure mode is talking about the partner function as an obstacle. Any phrasing where engineering is slow, design is precious, marketing is fluffy, or the business does not understand is fatal in this room, even when the supporting anecdote is true.

The second is credit. A candidate describing a cross-functional win in the first person singular throughout reads as the person who will take the peer's work to the executive review. The third is the decision with no partner in it: the candidate decided, then informed. The fourth is the absence of genuine disagreement, which peers read as dishonesty or as never having done work that mattered.

# How to use this bank

Copy the probes into the question bank section of your simulator prompt, identifiers exactly as written. Have the simulator play a specific partner function, not a generic interviewer: the same story lands differently depending on who is listening.

Start at difficulty tier one (T1), move to tier two (T2) once you can answer without a preamble, and reach for tier three (T3) probes only in the final two sessions before the round.

Prepare at least three stories in which you changed your position because a partner was right. Peers weight those more heavily than any story where you were right, and most candidates arrive with none.

Practise the vocabulary of the partner function. You do not need fluency. You need their terms used correctly enough that they can tell you have listened to people like them before. Getting a term subtly wrong is worse than not using it.

# The probes

## Collaboration

**PEER-01 · collaboration · T1**
"Walk me through how you and your closest partner in another function actually worked day to day."
*Strong answer contains:* concrete mechanics (a recurring session, a shared document, a defined handoff) rather than sentiment, and one thing the candidate changed at the partner's request.

**PEER-02 · collaboration · T2**
"Tell me about a project where the collaboration went badly. What was your part in it?"
*Strong answer contains:* a specific behaviour the candidate owns, what the partner experienced from their side, and the repair attempt rather than only the diagnosis.

**PEER-03 · collaboration · T2**
"When have you brought a partner in earlier than you needed to, and what did it cost you?"
*Strong answer contains:* the stage they were brought in at, what the early involvement changed about the outcome, and the acknowledged cost in time or in losing control of the work's shape.

**PEER-04 · collaboration · T2**
"How do you handle a partner who is not delivering, when neither of you manages the other?"
*Strong answer contains:* a direct conversation before an escalation, a check on whether the cause was capacity or priority rather than will, a shared visible commitment, and a stated escalation threshold with the partner told in advance.

**PEER-05 · collaboration · T3**
"Describe a time you were the reason a partner's work got harder."
*Strong answer contains:* an honest example (a late change, a missing decision, a shifting requirement), what it cost them specifically, and the practice the candidate adopted so it stopped recurring.

## Credit

**PEER-06 · credit · T1**
"Tell me about a result you are proud of. Who else made it happen?"
*Strong answer contains:* named roles and specific contributions offered without prompting, and a clear statement of the candidate's own part that neither inflates nor disappears.

**PEER-07 · credit · T2**
"Describe a time your work was presented by someone else, or someone else's work was presented by you."
*Strong answer contains:* how attribution was handled in the room, what the candidate did about it, and evidence they have a deliberate practice rather than a one-off reaction.

**PEER-08 · credit · T3**
"When did you take the blame for something that was not entirely your fault?"
*Strong answer contains:* the reason (protecting a junior person, owning the system rather than the incident), what was said privately versus publicly, and no expectation of reward for it.

## Disagreeing with a peer

**PEER-09 · disagreement · T1**
"Tell me about a time you disagreed with a peer and you were right."
*Strong answer contains:* their argument stated fairly and strongly, how the disagreement was resolved by evidence rather than by persistence, and how the relationship was left afterward.

**PEER-10 · disagreement · T1**
"Tell me about a time you disagreed with a peer and they were right."
*Strong answer contains:* what the candidate had missed, how quickly they changed position, whether they said so publicly, and what they now check because of it. Every candidate should have this story ready and most do not.

**PEER-11 · disagreement · T2**
"You and a peer both think the other's proposal will fail. There is no obvious tiebreaker. What happens?"
*Strong answer contains:* an attempt to find the smallest test that distinguishes the two, an agreed decision owner if no test exists, a documented disagreement, and full commitment once decided.

**PEER-12 · disagreement · T2**
"When have you escalated a disagreement with a peer to your managers? Was it the right call?"
*Strong answer contains:* what was tried first, whether the peer knew it was going up, the threshold that justified it, and an honest assessment including the case where it was premature.

**PEER-13 · disagreement · T3**
"A peer keeps relitigating a decision that has already been made. How do you handle it?"
*Strong answer contains:* a check on whether new information has actually appeared, a direct conversation about the pattern, a mechanism for legitimate reopening, and no public embarrassment of the peer.

## Working with engineering

**PEER-14 · engineering · T2**
"How do you respond when engineering says something will take much longer than you expected?"
*Strong answer contains:* asking what is driving the estimate before negotiating, separating scope from complexity from unknowns, offering a scope change rather than pressure, and no story where the estimate was talked down. Function-specific: aimed at non-engineers.

**PEER-15 · engineering · T2**
"Tell me about a time you changed a plan because of a technical constraint you did not anticipate."
*Strong answer contains:* the constraint described accurately, when it surfaced, what the candidate changed, and the practice adopted to surface such constraints earlier.

**PEER-16 · engineering · T2**
"How do you handle technical debt in a roadmap when it competes with visible features?"
*Strong answer contains:* an argument in terms of future delivery speed and risk rather than virtue, a specific allocation approach, and one example where the candidate protected the allocation under pressure.

**PEER-17 · engineering · T3**
"An engineer tells you the requirement you wrote is the wrong solution to the problem. What do you do?"
*Strong answer contains:* returning to the problem statement rather than defending the requirement, evidence the candidate has taken an engineer's alternative before, and the boundary where the product decision remains theirs.

## Working with design

**PEER-18 · design · T2**
"How do you give feedback on a design without designing it yourself?"
*Strong answer contains:* feedback framed as a problem or a user consequence rather than as a solution, a distinction between preference and evidence, and an example where the designer's answer beat the candidate's suggestion.

**PEER-19 · design · T2**
"Tell me about a time research or a design critique changed what you shipped."
*Strong answer contains:* what the candidate believed beforehand, the specific finding, the concrete change, and what it cost in schedule.

**PEER-20 · design · T3**
"A designer wants three more weeks for quality and the date is committed. Run that conversation."
*Strong answer contains:* asking what specifically improves and for whom, an attempt to split the improvement from the whole, a decision made jointly rather than announced, and a way the deferred work gets a real slot rather than a promise.

## Working with data and analytics

**PEER-21 · data · T2**
"How do you decide when an analysis is good enough to act on?"
*Strong answer contains:* a stated threshold tied to the reversibility of the decision, questions about the data's known weaknesses, and an example of acting on incomplete analysis with a tripwire attached.

**PEER-22 · data · T2**
"An analyst brings you a result that contradicts what you have been telling leadership. Walk me through the next conversation."
*Strong answer contains:* verifying the result with the analyst rather than around them, a fast correction to leadership, credit given to the analyst by name, and no pressure to re-cut the data.

**PEER-23 · data · T3**
"How do you avoid turning your data partner into a query service?"
*Strong answer contains:* bringing the analyst into the question-framing stage, sharing the decision context, protecting time for proactive work, and a specific instance where the analyst changed the question rather than answering it.

## Communication and handoffs

**PEER-24 · communication · T1**
"How do you keep partners informed without flooding them?"
*Strong answer contains:* a specific cadence and format, a rule for what gets pushed versus pulled, and an adjustment made because a partner said it was not working.

**PEER-25 · communication · T2**
"Tell me about a handoff that failed. What was missing?"
*Strong answer contains:* the specific missing context or artifact, the downstream cost, and the durable change (a checklist, a template, a joint session) rather than a promise to communicate better.

**PEER-26 · communication · T3**
"Describe explaining something in your domain to a partner who did not have the background, where it mattered."
*Strong answer contains:* the check on what the partner already knew, the analogy or framing used, the evidence that it landed, and an honest note about a first attempt that did not.

## Trust

**PEER-27 · trust · T2**
"What would your closest cross-functional partner say is frustrating about working with you?"
*Strong answer contains:* something a real person would say, delivered without defensiveness, plus the compensating mechanism. A polished non-answer here is worse than an unflattering true one.

**PEER-28 · trust · T3**
"If we hire you and in six months this is not working between us, what will it have been?"
*Strong answer contains:* a plausible friction rooted in the candidate's working style, the early signal they would want the peer to raise, and an invitation to raise it directly rather than through managers.

# The quiet question

Every probe above is a proxy for a question the peer will never ask: would I want to work with this person. It gets answered by texture rather than content, and it is worth knowing which textures move it.

Consider two candidates for the same product role at the fictional company Northwind Payments, interviewed by Priya Raghunathan, a staff engineer. PM Jordan describes a launch where the estimate came back at triple what they expected. They asked Priya's counterpart what was driving it, learned that half the cost was a data migration nobody had scoped, and cut the feature into two releases so the migration could be planned properly. The second candidate describes the same situation and says they pushed the team to find a faster path and it shipped on time. The second story may even be true, and it answers the quiet question in the wrong direction.

**What moves it toward yes.** Naming partners as people with legitimate constraints. Having at least one story where a partner changed your mind. Describing a decision you made jointly rather than announced. Knowing enough of the partner's craft to ask a real question about it. Owning a specific way you made someone's job harder.

**What moves it toward no.** Any hint that the partner function is an obstacle to be managed. First person singular throughout a team result. Perfect harmony in every story. Correcting the interviewer on their own domain. Certainty about a tradeoff that belongs to them.

**Ask them something real.** At the end, ask about their working life: what makes their work harder than it needs to be, what they wish their counterpart did differently, what the last hard disagreement in the team was about. It signals you are already imagining the Tuesday they are imagining.

# The follow-ups they will actually use

**"What did your partner think at the time?"** Asked whenever a story is told entirely from the candidate's point of view.

**"Who actually made that call?"** Tests decision rights and whether the candidate is claiming a shared decision.

**"What was their strongest argument?"** If the candidate cannot reconstruct it, they did not engage with it.

**"Did they know you felt that way?"** Asked when a candidate describes frustration that may never have been said out loud.

**"How did that land with them?"** Shifts the frame from what the candidate did to what the other person experienced.

**"When was the last time you were the problem?"** Direct request for self-implication. A deflection here is heavily weighted.

**"Would they say the same thing you just said?"** Tests whether the retelling would survive the other person hearing it.

**"What happened to that relationship afterward?"** Durability check. A resolved conflict that ended the working relationship is not resolved.

**"How would you have wanted them to raise it with you?"** Tests whether the candidate has thought about being on the receiving end.

**"What do you need from someone in my role to do your job well?"** A real question, not a trap. A candidate who needs nothing will not ask.

# Scoring dimensions for this round

**Partner respect.** Are other functions described as people with legitimate constraints and expertise, in the vocabulary they would recognize, with no trace of contempt.

**Credit accuracy.** Is contribution attributed honestly and unprompted, with the candidate's own part neither inflated nor erased.

**Productive disagreement.** Can the candidate disagree, state the opposing case fairly, resolve it with evidence, and preserve the relationship. Includes a real story where they were the one who was wrong.

**Self-implication.** Can the candidate name a specific way they made a partner's work harder, without a rescue clause attached.

**Workability.** Would the peer choose to be on a project with this person. Signals: joint decisions, early involvement, mechanisms rather than promises, and questions asked about the peer's real working life.

**Communication and presence.** Warmth without performance, listening that visibly changes the next sentence, and comfort being corrected on the peer's own domain.

# Landmines

**The obstacle framing.** Any version of engineering being slow, design being precious, marketing being fluffy, or the business not understanding. It ends the round even when the specific story is accurate.

**First person singular on a team result.** Describing a cross-functional outcome as though the partners were resources. The peer imagines the executive review where this happens to them.

**No story where you were wrong.** A record of disagreements the candidate always won reads as either selective memory or as never having done contested work.

**Announced decisions.** Stories where the candidate decided and then informed the partner. Peers listen for whether they will be consulted or notified.

**Fake harmony.** Claiming every collaboration went well. It reads as unwillingness to be honest, which is itself the thing being tested.

**Correcting the interviewer on their own craft.** Telling an engineer how estimation works, or a designer what good design is. Confidence is fine, jurisdiction is not.

**Vocabulary mistakes worn confidently.** Using the partner function's terms slightly wrong at high confidence. Not using them at all is safer.

**Mechanism-free promises.** Answering a collaboration failure with "we agreed to communicate more". No process changed, so nothing changed.

**Blaming the process.** Attributing a partner conflict entirely to a bad structure or a bad manager, with no personal contribution named.

**Hygiene questions only.** Ending with questions about tooling and ceremonies rather than how the peer experiences the work.
