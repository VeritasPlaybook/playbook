# 05: Recruiter Intake

This is the one prompt in the library not aimed at a research tool. It is a short email you send to a human being, and the highest yield per minute in the kit. No amount of research replaces a recruiter telling you the panel is three people, ninety minutes, and that the third is the hiring manager's skip level.

It takes four minutes to send and usually comes back within a day. Send it early, because the answer changes what you build. Half the work in a simulator is deciding who is in the room, and guessing badly means rehearsing for a round that is not happening.

The reply becomes the top of `_STATE.md` and triggers prompt 02, which you cannot properly run until you know who you are meeting.

---

# When to run this

Send it the moment a round is scheduled, before you run any research. If the round was scheduled in a call rather than by email, send it anyway as a short follow-up. If the recruiter is an external agency contact, still send it: they will have less detail but will chase the client for you. Skip it only when you already have written confirmation of the panel, the format, and the focus, which is rare.

---

# The prompt

Send it as a plain reply in the existing scheduling thread. Keep it short and keep the numbering, because numbered questions get numbered answers and prose questions get one vague paragraph.

```
Subject: Quick questions before [DATE] so I come prepared

Hi [RECRUITER FIRST NAME],

Thanks for setting this up. Five quick questions so I can use the time well on the day.

1. Who will I be meeting, and what is each person's function and relationship to the role?
2. What is the format and length, and is it one conversation or a set of back to back sessions?
3. What is this round specifically assessing? If there is a particular area the team wants to go deep on, I would rather spend my prep there than spread it thin.
4. Is there anything I should prepare, bring, or read beforehand: a case, a take home, a portfolio walkthrough, or a short presentation?
5. Where does this sit in the overall process, and what typically comes after it?

Happy with short answers to any of these. Looking forward to it.

Thank you,
[YOUR NAME]
```

Five questions is the ceiling. Longer lists get skimmed and half answered, and the two you most needed are the ones that get dropped.

---

# Why these are process questions and not product questions

Every question above is about logistics: who, how long, what format, what focus, what comes next. None of them ask what the company is working on, what the team's roadmap is, or how the product works.

That split is deliberate and matters more than it looks. Process questions cost the recruiter nothing, they are the recruiter's job, and they make you look organised. Product questions at intake go badly twice over: the recruiter usually cannot answer them, so you get a vague reply that helps nobody, and you have spent your one cheap early exchange on something you could have researched yourself. Worse, you have burned a good question that would have landed with the hiring manager in the room, where curiosity reads as interest rather than unpreparedness.

Keep the product questions. Ask them of the people who own the product, on the day.

---

# What to listen for beyond the answers

The reply carries more than its content. Read it twice.

**Level signals.** Watch how they describe the panel. A skip level or an executive on the list means the round is a calibration on scope and judgement, not a skills check. A peer from another function means cross-functional collaboration is being scored. If the panel is more senior than you expected for the stage, the role is probably being levelled up or down, so ask directly.

**Internal vocabulary.** Note the exact words they use for the round, the team, the level, and the process: "the loop," "the deep dive," "the panel," "hiring committee," "bar raiser," "final." Those words came from an internal document. Mirror them in your own emails and in the room. Add anything new to the vocabulary list in `Company and Role Brief.md`.

**Repeated candidate failures they hint at.** The most valuable sentence in any recruiter reply is unprompted advice. "They will want specific examples, not frameworks." "Candidates sometimes go too high level here." "Make sure you can talk through the technical detail yourself." Each of those sentences exists because someone recently failed that way. Treat it as a direct instruction and build a drill around it.

**Tone on timeline.** Urgency and pace tell you where you sit. A fast, flexible, we-can-work-around-you tone usually means you are a wanted candidate. A rigid slot with no movement and a slow reply often means you are one of several in a batch. Neither should change your preparation, but both should change how much leverage you assume later.

---

# Asking for more time without sounding like stalling

If the date does not give you enough preparation, ask once, early, and give a reason about quality rather than convenience. Late requests read as disorganisation. Early ones read as seriousness.

```
Hi [RECRUITER FIRST NAME],

One scheduling note. If there is flexibility, I would rather take a slot late next week than [PROPOSED DATE]. I want to spend proper time on [THE SPECIFIC THING: the case, the product, the technical prep] rather than turn up half prepared, and my current week is heavy.

If the timeline is fixed on your side, [PROPOSED DATE] works and I will make it work.

Thank you,
[YOUR NAME]
```

The last line is doing the work. You have asked without making it a condition, so there is nothing for anyone to push back on.

---

# The compensation question, handled carefully

If compensation has not come up and you need a range to know whether this process is worth your weeks, ask once, plainly, at intake:

```
One practical question so we are not wasting each other's time later: what is the budgeted range for this role at this level?
```

That is the whole question. Ask for their range, do not volunteer yours, and do not negotiate against it now.

The caution is real. Intake is where you have the least leverage you will ever have, because they have not decided they want you. Pushing on numbers before an offer turns a routine question into a signal that compensation is your primary interest, and recruiters screen on that. If the range is below what you need, say so once, calmly, without a counter-number: "That is below where I need to be, but I am interested enough in the work to keep talking if there is flexibility later." Then move on. The negotiation happens after they want you, and everything before that point should aim at getting them to that decision.

---

# What to do with the output

Paste the reply verbatim into `_STATE.md` under a **Recruiter intake** heading, with the date. Update the five round facts at the top of that file and remove the guess markers on anything the recruiter confirmed. Any unprompted advice becomes a line in the cheat sheet's landmines section. Any new vocabulary goes into the brief. Now that you know who is in the room, run `02 - Interviewer Deep Research` for each named person.
