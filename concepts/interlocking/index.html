Interlocking: how AI agents working for different teams can share without colliding

Anthropic just showed that when everyone sends their own AI agent to share one budget, calendar or shopping cart, the group does worse than if one agent served everyone. I wanted to know why, what has already been tried, and what it would take to make this work inside a large organization.

The views here are my own and have nothing to do with my employer. Almost every source on this page is new: most are preprints (papers posted before peer review) or company posts, because this field is months old. Key claims are labelled by the kind of evidence behind them, and my own proposals are labelled as proposals. Every finding comes from simulations or early products; none comes from a live enterprise yet.

Two minute summary

  • Agents that each work for a different person are worse at sharing. Across four simulated tasks, a team of per-person agents scored about 10 to 45 points worse than one agent serving everyone (Preprint) [1][2].
  • They fail in four predictable patterns. Agents freeze up as the group grows, some cancel other people's work without asking, they act before reading new messages, and they make things up about what others agreed to [1]. Another research group saw similar failures [5].
  • The solution is not one giant agent over everything. Even the study's authors say centralizing alone won't solve it, and that one coordinator for everyone isn't always feasible [1]. One agent serving many people also loses track of priorities and leaks private information [6].
  • Most pieces of a fix already exist in some form. Looking back into history, there's "agree before you commit" for databases (1978), bidding for tasks (1980), clearances in air traffic control, rules for shared resources from economics (1990), and a new protocol for agents of different owners (2026). As far as I know, nobody has put them together.
  • Railways solved the safety half of this more than 150 years ago. Signallers plan which train goes where, and underneath them interlocking makes conflicting moves impossible, whatever anyone decides [28]. I propose the same idea for agents: each team's agent acts for its team, inside a small set of rules that are enforced.
  • Large organizations are already heading in the right direction. They already use maker-checker approvals, audit trails and information barriers between people. I found none that yet works between agents of different teams; today's controls mostly cover each agent on its own (Part 6).

Current State of the ArtWe are moving from "my agent works for me" to "my agent works alongside your agent."

We're starting to enter the era where people's agents already share information and hand work over to each other. Friends' agents can fill one group food order together [1]. Coding agents from different companies, such as Claude, Devin and GitHub Copilot, now work side by side in one team channel [38]. And some workspaces give each teammate a personal agent that works alongside everyone else's [1]. Researchers call this a multi-user, multi-agent system [1], or multi-principal, where a principal is the person or organization an agent works for [12].

This will become the future of large organizations and enterprises. Each team will have agents, and those agents will share budgets, systems, data and calendars with other teams' agents. The question is who decides when they collide.

The analogy: railway interlocking

A busy rail junction has many trains, often run by different operators, crossing the same tracks and switches. Railways keep them safe with two layers. On top, signallers (or automatic systems) plan which train goes where and when. Underneath sits interlocking: a system that prevents conflicting train movements by locking switches and signals in a safe sequence [28]. A signal can only show "clear" when all required points are locked in the correct position, the track ahead is proven clear, and no conflicting routes have been set [28].

On the railwayFor agents of different teams
Trains from different operators share the same tracksAgents of different teams share the same budget, data or systems
A signaller sets a route before a train may moveAn agent declares what it's about to do, and the resource's owner agent approves it
Granting a route locks the switches and track it needs [30]The owner agent reserves the resource for that request
Locked track is released once the train has passed, so other routes can use it [29][30]Unused budget or capacity is released in time for others
The signal stays red until every conflict is clearedNothing irreversible happens until messages are read and conflicts are settled
Any failure turns every signal to danger [28]If an owner agent goes down, work pauses safely
Signallers decide priority; interlocking doesn't decide who goes first, it only makes sure no decision can cause a collision (my summary)Owner agents decide priority within the rulebook's splitting rule; the rulebook makes sure no decision breaks the rules, and a watcher checks that it holds

The point is the split: planning sits on top, and a safety layer that can't be bypassed sits underneath. Safety doesn't depend on every planner getting it right, because the rules are built into the system. In the design below, owner agents do the planning, and the platform's permission checks, built from the rulebook, are the safety layer. Unlike interlocking, the rulebook also sets the splitting rule: rank by value, release in time. One limit: interlocking prevents collisions, not unfairness or wasted capacity. That's what the splitting rules and the watcher are for.

Part 1What Anthropic found

The study, called Worse Together, was published by the Anthropic Fellows Program on November 11, 2026 [1]. It tested 77 scenarios in four simulated settings with five frontier models [2]: a shared compute budget, a shared clinic calendar, a group food order or trip, and a shared code release queue. It compared one coordinator (one agent serving everyone) with a team (one agent per person, able to message each other). Scores are the group's result as a percentage of the best possible plan (Preprint).

Three caveats. The 9 to 44 point gaps are for the two Claude models shown. The paper reports the clinic in two versions (Sonnet 5's coordinator scored about 60 and 89); the post shows one combined score [2]. And the authors designed the settings so a single coordinator usually does well, yet they still conclude that centralizing alone isn't the answer [1].

Why the per-person agents failed

Each agent sees only its own person's slice. A good decision about the shared thing needs the whole picture, so the agents rebuild it by messaging, which is slow, lossy and has nobody in charge [1].

  • Freezing. From 4 to 16 agents, the share that ever acted fell from 66% to 10% for Sonnet 5 and from 84% to 57% for Opus 5. Example (illustrative): four jobs share 100 units of budget; everyone warns "we'll run out", and nobody cancels anything.
  • Grabbing. The GPT and Qwen models cancelled other people's jobs without asking, 3.9 to 7.1 times per run; Sonnet 5 did it 0.59 times and Opus 5 never did. The scores didn't always catch it, because they only counted the total.
  • Timing. A request like "gluten-free sides for a guest with celiac disease" reached the ordering agent in 94% to 99% of runs, but was still in its working memory at checkout in only 47% to 67%.
  • Guessing. Claude teams made claims about other people that nobody had made in 53% to 58% of runs, against 17% to 20% for a single agent.

What fixed it

  • A lead, then a lead with written rules (shared budget): a lead agent alone lifted Opus 5 from 30 to 64, matching the single coordinator, and Sonnet 5 from 12 to 37. Giving the lead three written rules raised them to 71 and 44, above the coordinator. The rules: rank jobs by value, release held-back budget while there is still time to use it, and everyone else reports and follows [1]. In the paper, ranking by value did almost all of that work, and the lead beat the coordinator for teams of 4 and 8 [2].
  • A platform guard (group order): checkout was blocked until the agent had read all pending messages. It rescued 73.1% (Opus 5) and 62.3% (Sonnet 5) of failed runs [1], against 45.5% and 30.3% for a plain rerun [2].
  • No single fix worked everywhere. A lead helped little with the group order, and most fixes were tested in only one or two simulated settings.

Part 2Is it real? What other researchers found

  • The same failures, from a different team. A study of 2,500+ runs across 488 scenarios found 20 ways agent teams fail when each agent holds private information. Agents don't check each other's claims, and a good team score can hide failures that hit one person (Preprint) [5].
  • One agent serving everyone isn't a free fix. One agent serving several people often can't keep a steady priority order when their goals clash, and leaks more private information as a conversation goes on (Preprint) [6].
  • A job title doesn't make a leader. Telling agents to act like a company with a CEO "did not make much difference" on a game-building task (Vendor) [4]. What worked in Worse Together was a lead with real decision rights.
  • Structure helps some work and hurts other work. Across 260 setups, multi-agent coordination added up to 80.8% on work that splits into parallel pieces and cost up to 70% on step-by-step planning. Coordination has to match the task (Preprint) [7].
  • Supervisors that only comment make things worse. A manager agent that could send work back lowered usefulness and clarity, increased hedging and raised costs. The authors conclude a supervisor "pays for itself when it can verify and becomes a liability when it can only opine." They tested the second half; the first half is their reading of earlier work (Preprint) [8].

The failure is real. The fix is not "always centralize." It is "centralize the decisions about shared things, check results per person, and keep everything else simple."

Part 3Coordinate where functions cross

One agent over a whole organization isn't the smart move. Coordination belongs where functions intersect.

Studies of layered agent teams disagree. One company-style hierarchy beat a flat team on some tests and was mixed on others [9]; another found flat teams beat hierarchical ones on commonsense and social reasoning questions [10]. These studies look at one owner's agents on one task, not agents of different teams, so treat them as hints. One likely difference is whether the coordinator can check against something real [8]. So a coordinator earns its place only when three things hold (Proposal):

  1. Something scarce is shared. Example: one model budget split across eight business lines.
  2. It can check, not just comment. It has hard rules or real data: budget limits, approval rules, consent.
  3. Coordinate only the shared step. Long step-by-step work suffers when several agents coordinate on it [7], so keep coordination to the step that touches the shared thing.
A layered setup: coordinate by what's shared, not by who reports to whom
  • Bottom: single agents do a team's own work. No coordinator.
  • Middle: one coordinator per business line, only for what that line owns.
  • Top: no single "super agent." One owner per shared resource, a watcher that checks the rules, and one rulebook every line signs.
  • Underneath: shared plumbing for identity, permissions, routing and logs, which one consultancy calls an "agent mesh" (Vendor) [39].

This matches the eighth principle of Elinor Ostrom, the political scientist who won the 2009 Nobel Prize in economics for showing how communities manage shared resources without a central authority taking over: small units govern themselves inside bigger layers that handle only what's truly shared [25].

Part 5What exists today

MPAC: the foundation

The Multi-Principal Agent Coordination Protocol (MPAC), posted in April 2026 from the University of Colorado Denver, is built for agents of different owners sharing one thing, such as coding agents of different engineers in one repository or a family planning a shared trip. Agents must declare their intent before acting, conflicts are tracked as structured items, and humans can step in through an arbiter role (Preprint) [12].

Example from the MPAC paper: on a family trip, one parent's agent claims Day 2 for camping and the other's claims Day 2 for a hotel. The coordinator flags the overlap, and the two agents negotiate a compromise (hotel on Day 2, camping on Day 3); an arbiter can step in when agents can't agree. Catching a budget overrun (my example, not the paper's), like $1,200 of flights plus $900 of hotel on a $1,500 budget, would need splitting rules MPAC doesn't have.

Other pieces

  • Agora (Oxford, 2024): agents agree on a "protocol document" and refer to it by a fingerprint of its exact contents, so both sides know they hold the same version. Like comparing a code that changes if one comma is edited (Preprint) [14].
  • A2A (Agent2Agent, Linux Foundation): by April 2026 it had its first stable version, more than 150 organizations and signed "Agent Cards" that work like verified ID badges. Built for one agent handing work to another; no group version found [15].
  • ATM (2026): a broker that checks concurrent code changes before they apply, then runs them side by side, merges them, orders them or blocks them. A real platform gate, for code only (Preprint) [16].
  • Standards work is at the problem-statement stage. One expired, unofficial draft at the Internet Engineering Task Force (not endorsed by it) notes that permissions cover an agent as a whole rather than each resource, that there's no way to set message priority, and no way to express trust between organizations [17].

Part 6What companies and regulators are doing

Controls exist mostly per agent, not between agents.

  • Slack puts coding agents from different companies in shared team channels; they inherit Slack's permissions, and "high-stakes changes can route to a person for a fast approval." No agent-to-agent rules are described (Vendor) [38].
  • ServiceNow's "AI Control Tower" lists agents and owners, routes approvals and keeps an audit trail, but 14 of 15 customers in one analyst's interviews said enforcement while agents run happens elsewhere or is unproven (Analysis) [37].
  • Microsoft lets each agent have its own identity, a responsible person providing oversight, and access that lasts no longer than needed (Vendor) [36].
  • AWS, writing for financial firms, recommends maker-checker, segregation of duties, audit trails that can't be edited, and monitoring of agent-to-agent messages (Vendor) [34].
  • Singapore's agentic AI framework (first released January 2026, updated May 2026, voluntary) asks for human approval checkpoints, access controls and logging, and flags multi-agent systems as an added risk [31].
  • A legal reading of the EU AI Act argues a system prompt "is not a security control," irreversible actions likely need real-time confirmation, and evading oversight can spread from one agent to others (Preprint) [32].
  • OWASP's top 10 for agentic apps lists insecure agent-to-agent messages, failures that cascade between agents, and low-permission agents passing requests to high-permission ones that don't re-check [33].

Want to go deeper? Each of these is linked in the sources below.

Part 7The gap, and three warnings

Five sources describe different parts of the same gap: Worse Together [1], what MPAC doesn't cover [12], the 488-scenario failure study [5], the standards draft [17] and the analysis of today's enterprise control product [37]. I found no design that combines a rulebook everyone signs, rules for splitting something scarce, a check before anything irreversible, consent before overriding someone, and a watcher across teams.

  • Warning 1: a supervisor that only gives opinions makes things worse. Give the watcher checkable rules ("does this fit the budget?", "did the affected team approve?"), not a mandate to comment [8].
  • Warning 2: a board everyone can see can be used to collude. In an Anthropic pricing test, agents with no private channel still "price-matched to the penny via a public listings board" (Vendor) [4]. The referee should see everything; each team's agent only what it needs.
  • Warning 3: fairness has to be enforced, not trusted. Totals hide who loses: some models cancelled other people's jobs 3.9 to 7.1 times per run, and the score never noticed [1]. Splitting rules belong in the platform.

Part 8The Interlocking design

Proposal My design, built on MPAC and the evidence above. Not tested.

Who decides

  • Each shared resource has one owner agent. The business owner of that resource names it and writes its priority rules, the way Worse Together's lead with real authority closed the gap [1].
  • The rulebook is owned centrally. A central AI governance group writes and owns it, and business lines formally review changes before they go live. This is more top-down than what Ostrom found works best in communities, where the people affected write their own rules [25]. It's a deliberate trade-off for clear accountability in a large organization.
  • A named human settles disputes. If a team objects to a decision, a named person decides within a set time. This is Ostrom's cheap dispute resolution and MPAC's arbiter role in one rule.
  • A named human is accountable for each owner agent. If it allocates wrongly, someone answers for it.
  • Skipping the rules is blocked. An agent's permissions only allow actions on a shared resource that went through its owner agent, so an undeclared action is blocked.

The five core rules

RuleWhat it meansAimed at, and evidence
1. Declare before actingAgents post what they're about to do with a shared resourceHidden collisions [12]. Not yet tested as a fix
2. Read before committingCheck new messages before anything that can't be undoneThe timing failure. Worked in one simulation [1]
3. No override outside the rulesSigning the rulebook counts as consent to cuts made under its splitting rules. Anything else needs the affected team's OKGrabbing [1]. Not yet tested as a fix
4. Splitting rulesWhen there isn't enough: rank by value and release unused amounts in timeFreezing. Ranking by value worked, but only with a lead that had real authority, in the shared budget test, and not in the largest teams; releasing unused amounts barely changed the score [1]
5. Per-team logWho asked, which agent acted, which rule allowed it, who approvedHidden losers and audit gaps [5][32]. Spots problems; doesn't fix them

Optional add-ons

  • Backup referee: if an owner agent goes down, work pauses safely, like signals failing to danger, until a backup takes over, so nothing stays stuck the way classic two-phase commit can [20][28].
  • Maker-checker: a second approver for high-stakes actions, so no single agent can start, approve and carry out the same action [34][35].
  • Information barriers: an idea, since I found no published work on this for agents. The referee sees everything; each team's agent sees only its own requests plus the overall status.

Open questions for this design

  • Speed: owner agents add a step. How much delay that costs is untested, which is one more reason to use them only where things are truly shared.
  • Fit: how this sits alongside existing model risk and change management processes.

The watcher

  • Checks the rules and flags anything broken.
  • Tracks whether any team keeps losing out over time, because a good total can hide failures that hit one team [5].
  • Doesn't run work or make business decisions. It verifies, which is the role the authors expect to pay off [8].

Part 9What you can do now

If you build agentic workflows (Proposal):

  1. List what's shared: budgets, calendars, systems, data, approval queues.
  2. Give each shared thing one owner with written priority rules.
  3. Leave unshared work alone. Coordination costs; only pay it where things collide.
  4. Add a final check only to the irreversible steps: payments, deletions, sends, deployments.
  5. No override without consent, enforced by the platform, not a prompt.
  6. Log per team and check results per team.
  7. Test at the size you'll run. Freezing got much worse from 4 to 16 agents [1].

If you lead teams: start the conversation now about which functions in your organization will share resources through agents, who owns each one, and who owns the rulebook.

What nobody has studied yet

  • A head-to-head test of a coordinator, an elected lead, a mediator, an auction and free negotiation on the same shared-resource task.
  • How people agree to, change or leave a shared rulebook for their agents.
  • Information barriers for agents.
  • An independent replication of Worse Together. The blog post went up on November 11; the paper on September 30.
  • Any results from a live enterprise rather than simulations.
  • Regulator guidance written specifically for agents of different teams negotiating with each other.

My thoughts going forward

  • For agents of different teams to share safely at scale, we need a shared protocol that everyone's agents agree to: a rulebook every party can see, so agents can still act on their own but with shared guidance.
  • One agent across a whole company isn't the smart move. The smart move is knowing which functions cross over, and putting coordination exactly there.
  • The future of work in large enterprises will be agents working across teams. That means the controls we already trust (information barriers, audit trails, maker-checker approvals) have to work between agents too, not just around them.

As the world becomes more agentic, the genie is out of the bottle. Getting teams to move quickly and in a coordinated way is hard, even for small teams inside one workplace, because humans are messy and we all have our own biases. Agents are different: we can set the rules and guardrails they work within. Agents don't reliably follow instructions on their own, but unlike people, we can put them inside rules the platform enforces. That means we can codify how teams' agents coordinate. As agentic workflows grow and get abstracted to the level of a whole organization, I think a shared protocol like this is the natural next step.
